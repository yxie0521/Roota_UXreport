Abstract

10 interviews 31 survey responses 6 themes 6 evaluation participants

Roota is a mobile concept designed to help newly immigrant families in Australia discover, understand, and confidently join local community activities. Drawing on 10 semi-structured interviews and 31 survey responses, the project found that participation is blocked less by missing information than by uncertainty: families struggle to judge whether an activity suits them, face language and social anxiety, and rely heavily on familiar networks. Roota responds with centralised activity browsing, richer activity details, a comic-style scenario simulation, social-safety cues, and a child-friendly Kids Mode. A formative evaluation with six families completed all five tasks — validating the concept while surfacing clear directions for refinement.

Six key themes

The barriers form a chain.

Fragmented information leads to cognitive uncertainty, which collides with language and social pressure — each link makes the next harder to cross.

Theme 01

Fragmented information access

Activity information is scattered across schools, WeChat groups, social media and government sites, with no single entry point. Delivery is passive, not searchable.

“There is no way to know about these activities — I usually find out through school or friends.”Participant P1
Theme 02

Decision-making difficulty

Even when information is visible, families can't judge whether an activity suits them. Descriptions are brief, the process is unclear, and there's no basis for “is it worth it?”

“I'm not quite sure what this activity is for, so I won't consider joining.”Participant P5
Theme 03

Language barriers

Limited English confidence is more than a communication gap — it becomes pressure that holds families back even when they understand the activity.

“My English is not very good, so I don't dare to communicate.”Participant P3
Theme 04

Social & cultural anxiety

Unfamiliar environments and cultural difference create a sense of risk. Families feel safer participating where someone is already known to them.

“Strangers make me feel pressured — I'd rather participate with people I know.”Survey & interviews
Theme 05

Limited participation capacity

Work, childcare and low energy mean families won't actively search. Complex activities are abandoned; effort must be near-zero to convert interest into attendance.

“Limited time — we won't actively seek out activities.”Survey & interviews
Theme 06

Unmet needs

Families asked, unprompted, for one integrated platform, clearer descriptions, recommendations, others' reviews, language support and reminders.

“I want a platform to integrate all activities — and clearer information about what to expect.”Open-ended survey

03 · Design

From paper sketches to a warm, working prototype.

Low-fidelity wireframes mapped the core flows; a high-fidelity Figma prototype gave Roota its voice — calm, illustrated, and family-first.

Lo-fi → Hi-fi

Structure first, polish second.

Hand-drawn wireframes tested the skeleton of each screen — what information appears, in what order, and how families move from browsing to deciding, arriving, and checking in. Once the flow held together, a high-fidelity prototype layered in illustration, colour-coded suitability tags, and multilingual support.

The design principle behind Roota

Core features

Five moves that turn interest into attendance.

Feature 01

Activity browsing, in one place

A single home for everything happening nearby — replacing the scramble across schools, group chats and flyers.

  • “Near you” activities, sorted by relevance and distance.
  • A “For you” shelf and quick category filters (Sport, Art, Games, Food).
  • A one-tap language toggle — English / 中文.
No English neededFamily welcomeBeginner friendly
Live · tap a language
Feature 02

Activity details that support a decision

Not just what the activity is, but whether it fits your family — and what will actually happen on the day.

  • At-a-glance facts: date, location, cost and suitable ages.
  • A step-by-step “what will happen today” timeline.
  • Colour-coded suitability tags reduce guesswork for parents.
Decision supportCloses the “is it worth it?” gap
Try it yourself Feature 03

Scenario simulation

A comic-style rehearsal of the social moments of an event — so the first time never feels like the first time. Make a choice on the right to play through it.

  • Walk through arriving, first contact, joining in, and leaving.
  • Make choices and see warm, encouraging feedback.
  • Builds psychological expectations and reduces unfamiliarity.
No English needed“Like reading a comic” — P1

Natural Connector

You walked in like you belonged — and by the end, you did. Roota lets families rehearse these moments, so the first time never feels like the first time.

Feature 04

Friends Participate

Social safety, made visible. Seeing a familiar face going lowers the threshold to attend.

  • See who you know — and other families — attending.
  • Helpful tags: family details, languages spoken, dietary needs.
  • Leans on familiar networks — a key driver of whether families turn up.
Social trust“My vigilance drops a little” — P2
Flip the pages Feature 05

Kids Mode

A picture-book version of the activity, so children can understand and get excited — in their own language of stories. Turn the pages of the storybook on the right.

  • Warm, comic-style storytelling of what the day looks like.
  • Gentle badges celebrate participation, not addiction.
  • Lets children show interest; parents make the final call.
Picture-book style“The kind of style children love” — P1

Beyond the core

Maps, badges, points and a year-in-review keep families coming back.

What worked

The concept and its emotional features resonated.

Scenario simulation reduced fear

“It can greatly reduce my fear of this activity… the fear mainly comes from not understanding it” (P2). P1 likened it to a comic and a mini-game.

Friends Participate built social safety

Seeing a known family going lowered participants' guard — “my vigilance will also drop a little” (P2).

Kids Mode delighted parents

The picture-book style was seen as exactly what children would respond to (P1).

The aggregation concept landed

An integrated platform was recognised as genuinely useful for time-poor families (P2, P3).

What needs improvement

Clear, actionable gaps surfaced across the prototype.

Activity details lack practical detail

Families wanted check-in times, full address, parking, transport, and what to bring before committing.

Homepage discovery

The “View all” link was easy to miss; activities felt few and recommendations under-personalised.

Simulation depth

Choices didn't change outcomes; participants wanted branches and dynamic feedback.

Privacy & labels

The companion list needed visibility controls; labels like “Practice here” and “Let's go” were ambiguous.

Design recommendations

Six directions for the next iteration.

Sharpen discovery

Make “View all” prominent, expand the activity set, and personalise the “For you” shelf by family interests.

Detailed timeline & prep

Add arrival times, address, parking, transport, and a “what to bring” section to each event.

Expand the simulation

Let choices branch, add common social moments, and rename the entry to “Preview the activity”.

Privacy in the companion list

Clear “who can see my status” controls — show to friends, attending families, or keep private.

Age-based Kids Mode

Tailor content for 3–6, 7–9 and 10–12, add audio/animation, and a child lock with parent confirmation.

Balance the rewards

Keep badges as gentle encouragement that supports understanding — never the main reason to open the app.

05 · Reflect

The biggest lesson was that access to information is not the same as the ability to use it.

I began assuming the problem was discovery — that families simply couldn't find activities. The research reframed it. Families could often find something; what stopped them was uncertainty about whether it suited them, language and social pressure, and the absence of a familiar face. Good information design, I learned, has to support understanding, judgement and trust — not just presentation.

Working across interviews and a survey taught me to let qualitative depth and quantitative pattern check one another, and the formative evaluation reminded me that even a well-received concept hides concrete usability gaps. I'm also conscious of the study's limits: a small, predominantly Chinese-immigrant sample, a Figma prototype rather than a live product, and Kids Mode feedback gathered through parents rather than children. Future work should test with more diverse families and, ethically, with children themselves.

— Yani Xie, June 2026
